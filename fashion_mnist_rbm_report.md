# Fashion-MNIST Image Augmentation with Restricted Boltzmann Machines

## 1. Analogy
Imagine a large group of musicians in an orchestra who have never played together before, trying to tune their instruments and find a harmonious sound. Initially, they play notes at random, creating discord. However, by continuously listening to the musicians immediately around them and adjusting their own pitch back and forth, the group eventually settles into a stable, harmonious chord. A Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) operates on a very similar principle of collective negotiation. It consists of a network of interconnected units—split into visible units that see the data and hidden units that find patterns—that continuously adjust their states in response to one another. Through repeated, bidirectional feedback, the entire network settles into a stable, "low-energy" configuration that perfectly captures the underlying statistical harmony of the dataset. This makes RBMs highly effective at learning compressed, generative representations, allowing them to "play" entirely new data points that sound like they belong to the original dataset.

## 2. Detective
To understand why a Restricted Boltzmann Machine is the right fit for this specific image augmentation task, it is helpful to compare it against two common alternatives: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs).

*   **RBM vs. CNN (Pattern and Structure Reasoning):** CNNs are the industry standard for image data, but they are fundamentally discriminative models. They are highly optimized for drawing decision boundaries between classes (e.g., learning the exact pixel differences that separate a 'Pullover' from a 'Coat' or a 'Sneaker' from an 'Ankle boot'). However, because their goal is simply to classify, they do not inherently learn how to create new images. RBMs, on the other hand, are unsupervised, probabilistic, and inherently generative. Rather than simply memorizing boundaries, they learn the joint probability distribution of the pixels themselves. This structural focus allows an RBM to sample from its hidden layer to synthesize completely new, plausible examples of clothing, fulfilling the core requirement of our augmentation task.
*   **RBM vs. GAN (Data Size and Complexity Fit):** By slicing the dataset to the first half of its original order, we are left with exactly 30,000 training rows. Upon inspection, this subset remains remarkably well-balanced, containing roughly 2,900 to 3,000 images per class. While a GAN could theoretically generate high-quality augmented images, GANs are notoriously data-hungry and structurally unstable, frequently suffering from mode collapse where they forget how to generate certain classes entirely. Given that our dataset size is strictly limited to 30,000 examples, and the data type consists of structurally simple, low-resolution 28x28 grayscale images, a GAN introduces an unnecessary level of complexity and risk. An RBM offers a much more stable and computationally efficient alternative for a dataset of this modest size, reliably capturing the simpler feature space without the need for adversarial training dynamics.

## 3. Design
**Raw Data** ➔ **Preprocessing** ➔ **Architecture** ➔ **Output**

*   **Raw Data:** Fashion-MNIST first-half subset — Exactly 30,000 grayscale images. Dimensions are 28x28 pixels, with raw pixel intensity values ranging from 0 to 255. The data is distributed across 10 distinct classes: T-shirt/top, Trouser, Pullover, Dress, Coat, Sandal, Shirt, Sneaker, Bag, and Ankle boot.
*   **Preprocessing:** First, normalize the 0-255 pixel intensity values to a continuous [0, 1] range (and optionally binarize them, as standard Bernoulli RBMs operate on binary states). Next, flatten each 28x28 2D image matrix into a single 784-dimensional 1D vector. Split a small portion of the 30,000 images for validation monitoring.
*   **Architecture:** A Restricted Boltzmann Machine configured with a visible layer of 784 units (corresponding to the flattened input pixels) and a hidden layer of 256 units to force a compressed, generalized feature representation. The network weights are updated using the Contrastive Divergence (CD-k) learning algorithm.
*   **Output:** Synthetically reconstructed and augmented 784-dimensional vectors sampled from the trained RBM. These vectors are then reshaped back into 28x28 grayscale image matrices and added to the pipeline as synthetic training examples.
